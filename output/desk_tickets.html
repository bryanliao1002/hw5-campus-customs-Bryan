Reflection

Problem 10 · Lessons learned

What “resolved” means for an out-of-stock order

A ticket can reach Resolved after its latest agent workflow is complete, every financial proposal has a human outcome, and a human explicitly chooses what to do about any remaining fulfillment shortfall. For Ticket 101, the paid invoice does not prove a size-S Bulldog tee is available or delivered. A decision such as defer, backorder, or cancel records that the blocking issue was handled; it is not a shipment or delivery receipt.

The current system has no authorized way to confirm received goods, and this homework step must not simulate a delivery or increase stock. Any future receipt capability must be human-only: receive_purchase_order should record the actual quantity received against an approved purchase order and transactionally update inventory. Inventory should own the receipt record, with Accounting reconciling the PO and financial records. This tool is a documented gap only; it is not implemented or available in the current team.

Operational decisions are kept in an external, file-locked JSON record and the append-only audit trail, not in new shop-database tables or columns. The dashboard records the human's name, selected option, and terms; the MCP final-disposition check independently decides whether the ticket may be marked resolved.

1. How the agents handled these three tickets

Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee: The specialists did the useful investigation: Inventory established that the exact size-S tee had zero stock and treated Bulldog Print Co. only as a historical supplier candidate, Accounting checked invoice 501 and prepared the $840 proposal, and Customer Service saved a careful draft with no delivery promise. The weak point was Boss synthesis: one run failed at the request limit, another exceeded 12,000 tokens after the specialist reports were already saved. Once the human approved the invoice and chose to defer fulfillment until restock is confirmed, the Boss-only recovery reused those reports rather than repeating the work. I think that recovery was a good use of the audit trail. One Boss recovery summary had stale wording that the ticket was not marked resolved; I checked it against the later final-disposition event and database status, which both say resolved. The ticket is operationally resolved, but the tee is still not in stock and has not been delivered.

Ticket 102 — Rent due: This was the smoothest run. Boss brought in Facilities to confirm the $2,400 lease payment and due-date timing against the shop date. The agent workflow correctly stopped for a human; Bryan approved it, and the payment was recorded. The checking balance and lease due date then updated, and the final MCP check resolved the ticket. One useful detail from the audit is that Boss prepared the request itself after Facilities reported back; the expected plan had Accounting do that step, but no Accounting-agent run was recorded.

Ticket 103 — Bulk hoodie discount: The specialists found the important facts: 8 of 20 hoodies were available, 12 were short, and the recorded cost/list prices were $22/$58. Accounting correctly refused to invent a discount without a target price, and Customer Service saved a draft asking for one. This ticket also exposed our rough edges: a fulfillment decision was rejected because this is a discount ticket, Boss exceeded its token limit twice, and an early mark_ticket_resolved call properly left the ticket open while the workflow was incomplete. The later Boss-only recovery verified and reused the saved Accounting note, draft, and human decision. No discount was granted and the 12-unit shortfall was not filled.

At final review, the ticket tabs and screenshots showed all three database statuses as Resolved. The dashboard's run counter showed 00 and its activity panel was quiet after reload, because the live run objects are held in memory. I therefore used the append-only audit records to check the actual delegations, saved specialist reports, human actions, failures, recovery workflows, and final-disposition events instead of treating the quiet panel as proof that nothing had run.

2. Expected plans compared with what happened

For Ticket 101, the expected plan began with Inventory and then had Inventory bring in Accounting. The actual Boss did ask Inventory first, but Boss delegated to Accounting and Customer Service directly rather than having Inventory delegate. The first completed specialist run still covered the intended stock, invoice, payment, and draft work. The bigger difference was after that: Boss could not synthesize within the token budget, so the later recovery had to be added. That recovery was appropriately narrow and used the saved reports plus the human decisions.

For Ticket 102, the expected Facilities-first route happened. The expected next step was Facilities → Accounting; in practice Boss handled the exact $2,400 proposal itself after the lease and available-cash facts were returned. That was a reasonable shortcut for this simple, linked rent obligation, as long as the separate human approval remained in place—which it did.

For Ticket 103, our plan expected Inventory first, followed by Accounting and Customer Service. The first attempt did include Inventory and Accounting. In the later run, we streamlined the route: Accounting's MCP product-context tool already returned both stock and pricing, so Boss did not need to send Inventory to repeat that lookup. Accounting and Customer Service saved their work, but Boss still hit a token ceiling. The two failed synthesis records were kept; the final Boss-only recovery was a separate run, not a rewrite of those failures. We also learned to use the discount decision kind here rather than fulfillment.

3. Work a single tool-using agent could handle

Ticket 102 is the clearest example. One well-scoped agent with the rent-context, financial-context, and payment-request tools could verify the linked lease, compare its due date to desk.date_today, check cash, and prepare the exact proposal. Splitting that short chain between Facilities and Boss added coordination without much extra insight. I would still keep the human approval outside the agent.

Parts of Ticket 103 could also be combined: Accounting's product-context tool already returns stock and pricing, and the saved pricing note is a straightforward fact-based calculation. A single agent could do that analysis and recommend asking for a target price. Keeping Customer Service separate still helped, though, because writing a customer-facing draft is a different job and its no-promise wording matters.

Ticket 101 benefited more from distinct checks. Inventory had to reason about an exact SKU/size shortage and what vendor evidence did—and did not—prove; Accounting handled an invoice and cash proposal; Customer Service wrote a sensitive update. A single agent could call all those tools, but separate specialists made the risks easier to review. The main lesson is to delegate for genuinely different expertise, not simply to create more agent turns.

4. Three realistic problems this team could handle now

  1. A customer asks whether a particular size is in stock.

    Boss could route the ticket to Inventory for an exact SKU/size check and then to Customer Service to save a factual answer as a draft. The product-context and board-item tools support that path, and the draft would not be sent automatically.

  2. A linked supplier invoice is approaching its due date.

    Accounting could check the invoice, shop business date, checking balance, and pending requests, then prepare an exact invoice-payment proposal. A human would still have to approve it before cash leaves the account.

  3. A landlord sends a routine rent notice for a recorded lease.

    Facilities could verify the lease amount and due date using the stored shop date; Boss or Accounting could check funds and prepare the rent request. The approval route would record the payment and update the lease without the agents contacting the landlord.

5. Three problems we cannot safely solve yet

  1. Confirming that a shipment actually arrived and putting it on the shelf.

    We can prepare a purchase request, but the current tools cannot record a real receipt. We would need a human-only receive_purchase_order MCP tool that checks the approved PO and actual received quantity, updates inventory transactionally, and preserves receipt evidence. Inventory should own that step, with Accounting reconciling the PO and bill. Until then, neither a purchase approval nor a resolved ticket means goods were received.

  2. Sending an email or contacting a supplier.

    Customer Service can draft messages, but the project intentionally has no tool that sends them or calls a vendor. If the shop later authorizes real contact, we would need a tightly permissioned send/contact MCP tool with human review and delivery logs, plus a communications or vendor-relations agent to manage replies. That would be an expansion of the current no-external-contact rule, not something the existing agents should do.

  3. Recording sales income, customer refunds, or other money coming in.

    This homework's financial model only lets money leave the business, so these agents cannot reconcile a sale or refund into cash. That would require verified point-of-sale/refund records and MCP tools for incoming transactions and reconciliation, plus an Accounting workflow for matching them to the bank. It should be added only if the assignment's cash-flow rules are changed.

6. What the Cash tab taught me

The numbers made the human-approval rule feel concrete: checking started at $3,400; the approved Ticket 101 invoice payment took out $840, leaving $2,560; the approved Ticket 102 rent payment took out $2,400, leaving $160. Ticket 103 changed cash by $0. The two payment rows, approver names, invoice status, and final cash_accounts balance agree. We did not count a request as a payment, invent incoming revenue, or let the balance go negative.

My overall takeaway is that the team was strongest when its job was bounded by clear records and an exact MCP action, and weakest when Boss had to carry too much history into one final answer. Human choices and the final-disposition gate kept those weaknesses from turning into a duplicate payment, invented stock, or a false delivery claim. The recovery work made the system more useful, but the audit trail—not a green badge by itself—is what lets me explain why each ticket ended where it did.
